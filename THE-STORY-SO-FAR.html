The Story
So Far

One toy that evolves sound, and the three (soon four) instruments built to interrogate it. Where it started, what got made, what the numbers say, and where things stand as of 4 September 2026.

The story

First was Playing God — a way to evolve your own sounds across a truly vast phase space, with the only fitness function being how long you chose to listen to a given sound, or creature. A listener presses a key; a clock runs; the next keypress stops it; that duration is the entire signal. No ratings, no rankings, no taste declared out loud. The system is agnostic as to why you kept listening — it only cares that you did.

To make that work we had to build a creature genome: 64 richly parameterised waves and a handful of meta-genes covering how it evolves. A genome is a bank of oscillators that can modulate one another; render it and you get a sound. Mutate it, breed it, and the sound drifts. The bet was that somewhere in that space are sounds nobody could have specified in advance — and that listening time, and only listening time, would find them.

Which raised the obvious, dangerous question. Is the instrument any good? Can 64 waves actually say anything — a bell, a voice, a train — or does the format quietly cap what can ever be discovered? You cannot answer that by listening to random creatures. You answer it by pointing the engine at a known sound and asking it to reproduce it. That gave the sound the target the discovery toy refuses to have, and it spawned a family of programs, each one a different way of asking the same question.

Second came MIMIC — the discovery toy run in reverse. Same genome, but now with a target and a merciless, computed fitness, so it can grind autonomously with no human in the loop. It evolves toward the target, still blind, still by trial and error. Third came ARTISAN — sighted where MIMIC gropes. It measures the target with an FFT, takes it apart, and builds a genome on purpose. Where MIMIC feels its way to the sound, ARTISAN looks. And now a fourth is being drawn up — IMPRESSIONIST — which keeps ARTISAN's method but stops chasing the exact waveform and chases what the ear hears instead.

Four programs, one engine, two axes. That is the map.

The map

Four programs, one engine

Everything hangs off two questions: does the program search blind or design sighted, and does it chase the exact waveform or the ear. Playing God sits outside the grid — its judge is a living listener, the thing every other program's metric is only a proxy for.

Playing God

The toy that evolves sound

The generator is the whole point; everything else exists to test it. It is a near-empty page driven by single keys. SPACE draws a new creature. M plays a mutated child of the current one. B steps back to its parent. You never rate anything — you just listen, or you don't, and the clock does the rest.

The genome

A creature is one genome: 64 wave slots, each carrying 95 genes, plus 21 global genes that govern how the whole thing evolves. That is 6,101 parameters in total. But most of them are silent at birth — only about 162 actually shape the sound of a fresh creature. The rest is dark material, mutating freely at no cost, waiting to be switched on if selection ever finds a use for it. Complexity is not granted by the designer; it is unmuted when it earns its keep.

Vastness is the point

The governing rule of the whole project is a refusal. Priors are allowed to bias where the search looks first; they are never allowed to make any region unreachable. The space is an asset, not a cost — and it is difficult to overstate how large it is.

The genome space is larger by a factor of about 1014,613. Even the handful of expressed parameters in a newborn creature already spans ~10390 possibilities. Nobody can specify what wins in a space like this. You can only maximise it and explore.

We do not know what will score high. We can only discover that by maximising phase space and exploring it. Jon · 2026-08-30, the founding instruction

That rule has teeth. Three separate times during design, an assumption about what would score well got quietly written into machinery that was supposed to be neutral — an archive axis, a health diagnostic, a breeding threshold. All three looked like reasonable engineering. All three were caught on review and torn out. Three instances is a pattern, not a slip, and it is why the fitness function, the descriptors and the diagnostics are all forbidden from encoding any guess about the answer.

Where the toy stands

The generator is built and its first gate is passed. Gate 1a asks the only question a proxy metric could never answer honestly — is any of this worth hearing? — so it needs human ears. On the first audition, 13 of 38 random creatures held a listener past five seconds. Jon called it a pass. Creature 016 was "a beast"; 034 "magnificent." A first real session ran ~213 listens and the evolution was audible in practice. A v2 followed the same evening; a v2.2 is in flight, racing two archive designs against each other so the choice gets made on numbers.

MIMIC

The discovery toy, run in reverse

MIMIC gives the engine the one thing Playing God refuses it: a target, and a fitness function with an opinion. It renders a candidate genome, compares it to the target sample by sample, and scores the sum of squared error. Lower is better; zero is a flawless match. No human listens are spent — the whole thing computes, so it can run for a day unattended.

The metric is deliberately blunt. It punishes a sound that is right but quieter, or right but shifted by a fraction of a second, with no mercy and no re-alignment.

I want a blunt tool — if I just wanted fidelity then mp3 coding already exists. Jon · on why the fitness stays brutal

The silence floor

Here is the trap that makes this hard, and the reference bar printed in every run. A genome that renders pure silence scores a specific error — the target's own energy. Any sound that is worse than doing nothing scores above that floor. So "below silence" is the real bar: it means you are genuinely matching the sound, not just fading to quiet. On the hardest targets, blind search struggles to get meaningfully under it at all.

The algorithm race

Three search strategies were raced under an identical render budget — a textbook genetic algorithm, an island model with migrating sub-populations, and MAP-Elites repurposed as an optimiser. Decided by measurement, not argument. The plain GA won, five targets to one, and became the default. Its best result, on a known 6-wave sound, landed 35× below the silence floor.

ARTISAN

Sighted design: the upper bound

MIMIC stays blind. ARTISAN doesn't. It measures the target with an FFT — reading off frequency, phase and amplitude directly — then constructs a genome on purpose, one wave at a time, re-solving all the volumes for the best fit after each. No evolution. This matters because it separates two very different failures: if ARTISAN can hit a sound that MIMIC can't, the gap is a search problem, not a limit of the format. ARTISAN is the expert upper bound on what 64 waves can actually express.

v1 was too clever, too fast

The first ARTISAN was asked: with 24 hours of compute, how close can one genome get to an arbitrary sound? It piled up its best ~64 near-stationary sine waves in under ten minutes and stopped — leaving 23 hours 50 minutes of the budget unspent and almost every one of the genome's 95 genes-per-wave untouched. It never used the envelopes that let a wave move.

v2 fixed both gaps, and measured every change rather than asserting it. Waves now use their powers — each can fade, glide or repeat, kept only when it demonstrably lowers the error. On a clean decaying tone, that one change cut the error roughly 18,000-fold: a struck note that v1 approximated with a pile of stationary sines became a single wave that decays. And the whole budget gets spent — an anytime optimiser polishes and reallocates waves, killing the least useful and re-spending its slot on the biggest thing still unexplained.

The results

The natural way to read these is "how many times better than doing nothing" — the silence floor divided by the error. Higher is better. Every target improved on v1 and beat MIMIC's blind search decisively.

Not everything gets used. ARTISAN examined phase modulation, free modulator operators and mixed shapes, measured that none of them helped on the actual targets, and dropped each with a number attached. A capability tested and rejected on evidence is a job done — not a feature to bolt on because the brief mentions it. The one correction worth flagging: v1's report blamed a stubborn residual on hidden modulation; v2 measured that the target had no modulation at all — the structure v1 couldn't reach was a repeating gate, and naming it correctly is what let v2 recover it.

MIMIC ARTISAN

Running right now: does a head start help?

Live as of 4 Sep, evening

The two families are being tested against each other this weekend. Two identical 24-hour MIMIC runs on the Westminster chimes, differing in one thing only: one starts from ARTISAN's hand-built genome, the other starts from random noise. The question is whether a good sighted starting point actually helps blind evolution, or whether random search catches up given enough generations.

The road ahead

What's drawn up but not yet built

ARTISAN v3 — tracks and voices

The next ARTISAN stops analysing frequency-first and starts analysing time-first: follow each partial as a track that is born, moves and dies, the way speech researchers have since the 1980s (McAulay–Quatieri sinusoidal modelling; Serra & Smith's spectral modelling synthesis). And it lets one wave slot serve several sounds in sequence — a voice gliding silently from one note to the next — because on real audio, slots are the binding constraint. Jon proposed this reluctantly, worried that writing it down would turn it into "a method" and rob the tool of its generality. The brief honours the fear: nothing in the code may know what a "note" is. It has to work as well on birdsong and a train as on chimes.

IMPRESSIONIST — chasing the ear

Every ARTISAN result hits a broadband floor — the strike transients, the reverb, the breath in a voice — that oscillators cannot match sample-by-sample. But that residual is exactly SSE's blind spot, not the ear's. A listener doesn't need the exact noise waveform, only noise with the right shape. IMPRESSIONIST keeps ARTISAN's sighted method but swaps the metric for a perceptual one (its nearest published cousin is DDSP, 2020). The catch is named up front — the metric is itself a proxy, so it must be validated against Jon's actual ears before it's trusted, or the whole thing just games a new number.

IMPRESSIONIST·MIMIC — on hold, by design

The fifth program fills the last empty corner of the map: blind evolution judged by the ear. It is deliberately on hold until the perceptual metric exists and has passed its ears-test — and it comes before IMPRESSIONIST on purpose, because blind evolution is a relentless machine for finding whatever a metric rewards, which makes it the perfect red team for catching a bad metric cheaply.

The through-line hasn't moved since the first day. Playing God is the point; MIMIC, ARTISAN and the two to come are all instruments for asking whether the toy's 64 waves are expressive enough to be worth pointing a listener at. So far the answer keeps coming back yes — sighted design can pull a bell, a voice, a known creature out of the format cleanly, which means the discovery toy is working with a rich enough vocabulary. The gap that remains between "sample-exact" and "sounds right" is not a flaw in the engine. It's the next program.
